OpenHamShack ATV {{VERSION}}

An analogue amateur-television transceiver for a Linux laptop: 625-line PAL with sound, generated, transmitted, received and decoded in one program, with a Pluto-family SDR.

A sibling of OpenHamShack QO-100 WB (DATV, in a box) and OpenHamShack QO-100 NB (QO-100 narrowband) and OpenHamShack Radio (all bands) — this one sends pictures the old way. By GM4BRI. Back to brian-henderson.co.uk.

Status Early preview On air at the bench

The whole chain is verified against the program's own simulated radio: the PAL waveform is measured against the broadcast standard and pictures go through encoder, modulator, a noisy channel, demodulator and decoder in the automatic tests. It has now been on the air at the bench, by GM4BRI: a Pluto nano on USB and a LibreSDR on Ethernet, each transmitting to the other on 23cm with the antennas a short distance apart. Pictures have come off the air in FM-ATV colour with sound (LibreSDR to nano at 12.5 MS/s: SNR about 24 dB, sound 47 dB SINAD), in AM-VSB colour at 10 MS/s, and in both narrow formats from the nano's 5 MS/s (Mini-MAC: SNR about 30 dB, colour, digital sound at 39 dB SINAD). That is two radios on one bench, not a contact over a real path. A stock ADALM-Pluto, the Pluto+ and the HackRF are untried.

  • Transmitting needs an amateur licence, and you are responsible for what you transmit: the band, the power, the bandwidth and your ident. The program's guard helps; it does not hold your licence.
  • A real radio does not transmit until you tick Let a real radio transmit in Settings › Transmit. The simulator transmits nothing at all.
  • An SDR's output is not filtered: a band-pass or low-pass filter after it (and after any amplifier) is needed before an antenna.

What it does

  • Two systems. AM-VSB, UK System I-style: negative-modulation PAL with a vestigial lower sideband, 5.5 MHz video, FM sound carrier at +6.0 MHz. FM-ATV to IARU Region 1 V.2: 3.5 MHz peak deviation, CCIR 405 pre-emphasis, FM sound subcarrier at 6.0 MHz (5.5 and 6.5 MHz selectable).
  • Three bands. 23cm and 6cm (5.8 GHz) for FM-ATV; 23cm also for AM-VSB; 70cm for AM-VSB, with a warning that analogue ATV is no longer in the bandplan there (below).
  • Its own picture generator: a test card with your callsign and locator in it, colour bars, a webcam, or a video file (anything ffmpeg plays, with its sound; File… opens where you last chose one, and names it). Sound: a 1 kHz tone, the microphone, the file's sound, or none. Your callsign is in vision on every source for the whole transmission and cannot be turned off.
  • Its own decoder: sync recovery, PAL colour and sound, with a waveform monitor (any line of either field) and a vectorscope; lock, SNR and line-jitter readings; a picture snapshot to PNG.
  • What fits: for the radio's sample rate, the card says what the signal occupies, how much video bandwidth, whether colour and sound fit — and what was given up when they do not. Transmit is refused when the signal does not fit.
  • Spectrum and waterfall of the receive band, with the bandplan strip, the channels to click, and the occupied bandwidth drawn over the signal; while transmitting on one radio, the signal being sent.
  • Colour and sound from a USB Pluto, to another OpenHamShack ATV: Mini-MAC (time-multiplexed colour with digital sound, after the MAC system of 1980s satellite TV) and narrow colour PAL (colour on a 1.6 MHz subcarrier, sound on 2.2 MHz) both fit the 5 MS/s a Pluto can transmit over USB. Neither is a standard, and the window says so wherever one is chosen; the receiver recognises them by itself. Both have been sent and received on the air.
  • The radio's own filters, undone. Every AD936x has fixed half-band filters that cut the top of the channel — at 12.5 MS/s, 7.8 dB off a 5.5 MHz sound subcarrier, transmitter and receiver together. OpenHamShack ATV models them from Analog Devices' own coefficients, and each radio undoes its own share inside filters the program already runs, at no processing cost. On the air the colour burst came up from 0.73 to 0.90 of what was sent. A filter left loaded by another program (a DATV application's) is bypassed while OpenHamShack ATV has the radio and put back afterwards.
  • It checks the radio does what it was told (from the release after 0.1.7): the Pluto driver reads back the rate, bandwidths, gains and LOs it sets, and watches the rate and LOs once a second while running. A value that moves is set back and the window says so in red; while transmitting, a transmit LO that moves unkeys at once. Found because a nano twice changed its own rate and LO under the program.
  • IQ recording and playback: Record IQ saves the radio's raw samples as SigMF, and a recording plays back through the same receiver, never transmitting.
  • A simulated radio is the default: stations on the bandplan channels and your own transmission looped back, so every mode can be tried with no hardware and nothing transmitted.

Radios, and what they can honestly carry

Analogue TV is wide. The limit is rarely the radio chip; it is how fast samples get between the radio and the computer. Colour AM-VSB with sound needs 10 MS/s; standard FM-ATV needs 20 MS/s.

  • Half duplex on a single radio over USB: receive pauses while you transmit, the picture shows what you are sending, and the spectrum shows the signal going out, in red, labelled TX. With a HackRF transmitting, the receiver keeps running.
  • 6cm: a stock Pluto (AD9363) tunes 325–3800 MHz. It needs the AD9364 setting, or an AD9361 board such as the LibreSDR, to reach 5.8 GHz; performance there is not guaranteed by Analog Devices, and output is about −3 dBm. The HackRF tunes 1 MHz–6 GHz but its output is also low at 5.8 GHz. An external PA and LNA are needed in practice.
  • At 23cm a Pluto gives about +2.5 dBm: a PA for any real distance, and an LNA and band-pass filter are recommended.
  • Each radio is steered to its best mode the first time it is used — 5 MS/s and reduced FM deviation for a USB Pluto (or, with 8-bit receive, colour reception at 10 and 12.5 MS/s), 16 MS/s for a LibreSDR, full width for a faster Ethernet radio or a HackRF — and the window says what it chose. Your own changes afterwards are kept.
  • Find in Settings › Radio lists the Pluto-family radios on USB and the network; choosing one makes it the radio, which comes up receiving, never transmitting.
  • The window says all of this where it applies: a band the radio cannot tune is greyed with the reason, and a sample rate above what the link sustains is marked.

70cm: outside the bandplan

Analogue ATV was removed from the RSGB 70cm bandplan on 4 December 2020, and IARU Region 1 has not permitted it in 430–440 MHz since 1 January 2021; only narrow DATV around 437 MHz remains. The UK licence itself does not forbid it, so OpenHamShack ATV allows AM-VSB on 70cm with a prominent warning, keeps the whole signal inside the 430–440 MHz allocation (433 MHz is offered as the channel where an AM-VSB signal with sound fits), and records each 70cm transmission with that warning in its transmit log. Receiving on 70cm is unaffected. Whether to transmit there is your decision, under your licence. 23cm is the place for AM-VSB transmit inside the bandplan.

Installing

One .deb for every architecture: the program is pure Python. It needs Python 3.11 or newer.

Debian 13 (trixie)

  1. Download the .deb above and check it against the SHA-256 shown: sha256sum openhamshack-atv_*_all.deb.
  2. Install it with sudo apt install ./openhamshack-atv_<version>_all.deb — using apt install ./… rather than dpkg -i lets apt pull in the required packages (python3-numpy, python3-pyqt6, python3-pyaudio) in the same step, and the Recommends by default: python3-libiio for the Pluto family, avahi-daemon for finding one on the network, ffmpeg for the webcam and video-file sources, and libhackrf0 for a HackRF.
  3. Run OpenHamShack ATV from your applications menu, or openhamshack-atv from a terminal. It starts on the simulator until you choose a real radio in Settings, so there is always something to see and nothing transmits by accident.

Ubuntu 24.04 or newer

The same command. PyQt6 and PyAudio are in Ubuntu's universe component; if apt cannot find them, run sudo add-apt-repository universe first. Ubuntu 22.04's Python 3.10 is too old.

Raspberry Pi OS (Bookworm, 64-bit)

The same command; every dependency is in the Raspberry Pi OS repositories. Not yet tried on a Pi, and its speed has not been measured: expect a Pi to manage the lower sample rates and to struggle with FM-ATV at 20 MS/s. The status bar shows skipped frames when the computer cannot keep up.

Built and tested on Debian 13. Debian 12, Ubuntu 24.04 and Raspberry Pi OS meet the package's dependencies but have not been tried. A missing optional package disables just that one feature, with a sentence saying which package to install — it never stops the program.
