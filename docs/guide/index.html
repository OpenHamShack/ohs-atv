1. What OpenHamShack ATV is

OpenHamShack ATV sends and receives analogue fast-scan amateur television: 625-line PAL pictures with sound, the way ATV has been done since long before digital. One program on a Linux computer does all of it — it draws the picture, puts your callsign in it, modulates it and sends it out of a software-defined radio; and it receives, demodulates and decodes pictures from other stations, with the instruments to see how good they are.

SystemWhat it isBands
AM-VSBPAL on an amplitude-modulated carrier with a vestigial lower sideband, as UK System I broadcast television was: negative modulation, 5.5 MHz video, a 1.25 MHz vestige, an FM sound carrier 6.0 MHz above vision.23cm; 70cm (outside the bandplan, see 8.1)
FM-ATVPAL frequency-modulating the carrier, to IARU Region 1 Technical Recommendation V.2: 3.5 MHz peak deviation (7 MHz peak to peak), 5 MHz video, CCIR 405 pre-emphasis, and an FM sound subcarrier at 6.0 MHz.23cm, 6cm

It works with the Pluto family of SDRs — ADALM-Pluto, Pluto+, Pluto nano and LibreSDR — through libiio, and can use a HackRF One as the transmitter while a Pluto-family radio (or the simulator) receives. Until you choose a radio, it runs on a simulated radio: stations on the bandplan channels and your own transmission looped back to you, so everything in this guide can be tried at the desk with nothing connected and nothing transmitted.

What it does not do

  • Digital ATV (DATV) — DVB-S and DVB-S2 are what the sibling programs, OpenHamShack DATV and OpenHamShack QO-100 WB, are for.
  • 2m talkback. ATV contacts are usually arranged on 144.750 MHz; use a separate rig.
  • Recording received video to a video file. You can save a still (Ctrl+S), and record the radio's raw IQ to play back through the receiver later (6.6).
  • Control an amplifier or a PTT sequencer. There is no PTT output; if your PA needs switching, do it yourself, and key the PA before the program and unkey it after.

2. Installing

OpenHamShack ATV is one .deb package for every architecture — it is pure Python — and needs Python 3.11 or newer. It is built and tested on Debian 13.

Debian 13

  1. Download openhamshack-atv_<version>_all.deb from the website, and check it against the SHA-256 printed there: 
    sha256sum openhamshack-atv_*_all.deb
  2. Install it with apt, from the folder you saved it in (the ./ matters): 
    sudo apt install ./openhamshack-atv_<version>_all.deb
    apt install ./… — rather than dpkg -i — fetches what it needs from your normal repositories in the same step.
  3. Start OpenHamShack ATV from the applications menu (Ham Radio), or run openhamshack-atv in a terminal.

Ubuntu 24.04 or newer

The same command. PyQt6 and PyAudio are in Ubuntu's universe component; if apt says it cannot find python3-pyqt6, enable it first with sudo add-apt-repository universe. Ubuntu 22.04 is too old: its Python is 3.10.

Raspberry Pi OS (Bookworm, 64-bit)

The same command; every package it needs is in the Raspberry Pi OS repositories. It has not been tried on a Pi, and how fast a Pi runs it has not been measured. Expect the lower sample rates to be comfortable and FM-ATV at 20 MS/s to be beyond it; the status bar says when frames are being skipped (section 10).

Untried distributions Debian 12, Ubuntu 24.04 and Raspberry Pi OS meet the package's dependencies, but only Debian 13 has been used. If something fails on another, the terminal output of openhamshack-atv is what to report.

To remove the program: sudo apt remove openhamshack-atv. Your settings and snapshots are in your home folder (section 16) and are left alone.

3. First run

The first time it opens, OpenHamShack ATV is on the simulator, on 23cm FM-ATV at 1255 MHz, and within a second or two shows a simulated station's test card, locked in colour. Nothing is transmitted — the simulator has nothing to transmit with — and nothing will be until you ask.

Before you can even transmit into the simulator, tell it who you are. Open Settings (the gear at the top right, or Ctrl+,) and on the Station page:

  • Callsign — drawn into every picture you transmit. Transmit is refused until it is set.
  • Locator — your Maidenhead locator, shown under the callsign.
  • Second ident line — anything short: your name, the band, the location.
  • Ident position — which corner the callsign box goes in on webcam and video-file pictures. The test card has it built into the middle.
  • Licence — tick I hold an amateur licence, I will identify, and I accept that I am responsible for what is transmitted. Transmit is locked until this is ticked.

Press Save. Now press PTT (or hold Space): on the simulator your own test card goes round the loop and comes back as the received picture, with your callsign in it. That is the whole chain — generator, modulator, a noisy channel, demodulator, decoder — working on your machine.

Settings never transmit Saving Settings restarts the radio when it has to (a new radio, sample rate or sound device), and it always comes back receiving. Nothing in the Settings dialog can key the transmitter.

4. Choosing a radio and its settings

4.1 Which radio does what

Analogue television is wide: a colour AM-VSB picture with sound needs a sample rate of 10 MS/s, and standard FM-ATV needs 20 MS/s. The radio chip in every Pluto-family SDR can do that; the link to the computer often cannot. Choose by what you want to send:

RadioLinkSustainsAM-VSBStandard FM-ATV
ADALM-Pluto, Pluto nanoUSB 2receive 6.25 MS/s in full (usb:), about 15 with Tezuka's 8-bit receive; transmit about 5.2; 5 MS/s through the USB network addresssends mono, no sound, 2.4 MHz video; receives colour and sound with 8-bit receiveno; narrow FM, and colour and sound to another OpenHamShack ATV (10)
LibreSDRGigabit Ethernet16.2 MS/s measured (its README says 20) colour and soundat 4 MHz p-p, 16 MS/s, with colour and sound
Pluto+Gigabit Ethernetabout 28 MS/s ceilingcolour and sound yes
HackRF One (transmit only)USB 2, 8-bit20 MS/scolour and sound yes

The Pluto nano and LibreSDR figures are measured with this program on a test laptop (an i5-1135G7). The nano receives 6.25 MS/s of 16-bit samples in full over libusb, but transmits only about 5.2 MS/s over USB; the radio runs one rate both ways, so to transmit it runs at 5 MS/s. On Tezuka firmware its packed 8-bit receive carries about 15 MS/s, enough to receive colour and sound, but there is no 8-bit transmit. The LibreSDR tested sustained 16.2 MS/s, not the 20 its README gives. The Pluto+ figure is a reported ceiling, not a sustained rate, and has not been tried. A HackRF carries 8-bit samples, half the bytes, so 20 MS/s fits USB 2.

So, in practice:

  • Pluto on USB alone: send a mono AM-VSB picture or narrow FM-ATV (1.2 MHz peak to peak, mono, no sound — section 10) that a standard FM-ATV receiver will show as a weak, low-contrast picture; or, to another OpenHamShack ATV, colour and sound in the same channel with Mini-MAC or narrow colour PAL. With 8-bit receive it receives AM-VSB at 10 MS/s and FM-ATV at 12.5 MS/s in colour with sound.
  • LibreSDR or Pluto+ on Ethernet: colour and sound both ways, on one radio. A LibreSDR at 16 MS/s sends FM-ATV at 4 MHz peak to peak rather than IARU V.2's 7 MHz, which needs 20 MS/s.
  • Pluto (USB) to receive and a HackRF to transmit: standard FM-ATV out, and receive carries on while you transmit; but a USB Pluto still cannot receive standard FM-ATV (see section 11).

It happens once per combination: anything you change afterwards stays changed until a different radio appears. To stop it altogether, turn off Choose the best mode for each radio in Settings › Radio.

4.4 The simulator

Settings › Simulator sets the simulated carrier-to-noise ratio (default 32 dB), whether other stations appear, and how far off frequency your own looped-back signal arrives. The simulated stations are an AM-VSB card on 433 MHz, FM-ATV on 1255 MHz, AM-VSB on 1275 MHz, an FM repeater output on 1310 MHz and FM-ATV on 5665 MHz. Lower the CNR to see what a weak signal does to the picture and the readings.

4.5 Checking the radio does what it was told

On 5 October 2026 the test nano was twice found at 2.331 MS/s and 1254.667 MHz while the program had set 10 MS/s and 1247 MHz; once it happened in the middle of a receive run, which stalled. Something outside the program set it (Tezuka's own control script can), and the window went on showing the figures the program had asked for. So, from the release after 0.1.7, a Pluto-family radio is checked:

  • When it opens the driver reads back what it set — the sample rate, both RF bandwidths, the transmit attenuation, the receive gain mode (and gain) — and at every tune, the LO. A value that differs is set once more and noted if it then holds. A radio that still will not hold it is refused, with a sentence giving both figures: The radio will not hold its …: set to …, it reads ….
  • While it runs, once a second, the rate, the receive LO and (while transmitting) the transmit LO are read again. A moved rate or receive LO is set back once; the "What fits" card shows in red The radio's … moved from … to … by itself; set back, it held, or that it will not go back. While transmitting, a transmit LO that moves unkeys at once — it would be transmitting off frequency — and so does a rate or receive LO that will not go back. The reason is written to the transmit log.
  • The 8-bit receive check is made twice before falling back to 16-bit: twice on 5 October the first check failed and simply opening the radio again found it.

Tried on the nano by moving its rate and LO from outside while it received: each move was caught within a second and set back, and receive recovered by itself. The reads cost a few milliseconds a second on a thread of their own.

  6. The radio: which, how it is reached, decoded frames per second and CPU. Click it to open Settings › Radio.
  7. Keyboard shortcuts (?), light/dark theme, and Settings.

Over the TX preview every label — TX PREVIEW, ON AIR and that one — moves aside from your ident, in whichever corner it is, so you can always read both of its lines.
  9. Received / TX preview switch.

  11. Vectorscope: the colour, as phase and amplitude, with the targets for the six colour bars.

  14. Transmit: the outgoing picture as it will be sent (OUTGOING; ON AIR while transmitting) and the transmit frequency.
  15. Picture and Sound sources (section 9).
  16. Ident: your callsign box exactly as it will appear in vision. Always on.
  17. Power (TX gain): drag to set; the red notch is the ceiling set in Settings, which the slider cannot pass.
  18. PTT. Click to transmit and click again to stop, or hold Space. When transmit is refused it reads TRANSMIT LOCKED, and the reason is written under it.
  19. Warnings about this frequency: bandplan overlaps and the like (7.6). On 70cm a red OUTSIDE THE BANDPLAN banner comes first.

The "What fits" card

Between the scopes and the transmit panel, this card says what your signal is at the current sample rate:

  • Occupied bandwidth on air, Video bandwidth, Colour (PAL, MONO, MAC or PAL 1.6), Sound (the carrier offset, the subcarrier, DIGITAL, or NONE).
  • A strip showing the sample band with the carrier (C), the PAL subcarrier and the sound (S) in it.
  • ✓ The signal fits this rate — or ✕ The signal does not fit this rate: transmit is refused — and in plain sentences what was given up.
  • Marks when the link cannot keep up: This link is reported to sustain … MS/s — expect dropped samples, Samples are being dropped, and HackRF underruns.
  • In red, a radio that changed its own rate or LO (4.5): for 30 seconds if it was set back and held, for as long as it did not.
  • With a narrow format, an amber line saying it is OpenHamShack ATV only — and, if the receiver found a different format on the air, Receiving … — found on the air. Transmit stays …. While you are not transmitting the card describes the format being received; while you transmit, the one you send.

Smaller screens

The window is laid out for 1920 × 1080 and also works at 1366 × 768: the side columns narrow, the radio's name moves to the status strip, the channels go four to a row, and the transmit panel shows the first warning with a count of the rest (hover for all of them).

6. Receiving

6.1 Tuning

  • Band and system: pick them at the top. Changing band moves receive and transmit to the band's usual frequency (70cm 437 MHz, 23cm 1255 MHz, 6cm 5665 MHz).
  • Channels: click one on the band card, or its marker on the spectrum.
  • Spectrum: click anywhere to tune there (rounded to 250 kHz, or the step if it is larger); the wheel over the spectrum steps by the tuning step.
  • Keyboard: ← → one step, with Shift ten steps.
  • Type it: click the frequency (or Enter when it has focus) and type 1255, 1311.5, 1255.25 MHz or 5.665 GHz. A frequency in another band moves to that band.
  • Step: 10 kHz, 25 kHz, 100 kHz (default), 250 kHz, 500 kHz or 1 MHz. Click STEP or turn the wheel over it.

Tuning with the wheel or arrows stops at the band edge rather than leaving the band.

6.2 Reading the picture

The decoder looks for line sync, then field sync, then colour burst. The badges follow it:

What you seeWhat it means
NO SIGNAL, grain and a rolling barNo sync found, or frames have stopped arriving. The picture never freezes on an old frame: if it shows a picture, the picture is live.
SYNC under 100 %, FIELD not lockedA weak or noisy signal: some lines' sync is lost.
SYNC 100 %, FIELD LOCKED, COLOUR MONOA good black-and-white picture: no colour burst, or the sample rate is too low for colour (the "What fits" card says which).
COLOUR PALColour decoded. The vectorscope's dots should sit on its targets for colour bars.
SNRUnweighted video SNR in dB, measured on lines that carry no picture. Higher is a cleaner picture.
JITTERLine-timing jitter in nanoseconds: how much the line sync wanders. Lower is steadier.

For AM-VSB the status strip also shows the measured carrier offset: how far the station's vision carrier is from where you are tuned. Inverted video on FM (sync upwards) is a setting both ends must agree on; see section 13.

6.3 The instruments

The waveform monitor shows one line (100 by default) in volts: sync tip at −0.3 V, black at 0, peak white at 0.7 V, the colour burst just after sync. On the test card you will see the greyscale steps. The arrows beside LINE, or the wheel over it, move one line; with Shift, ten. Lines 1–312 are field 1 (F1), 313–625 field 2 (F2).

The vectorscope plots the decoded colour. Colour bars land on six targets — R, MG, B, CY, G, YL — and a picture with no colour is a dot in the middle.

6.4 Saving a picture

Click the camera on the picture, or press Ctrl+S. It saves what the monitor shows at 768 × 576 as a PNG in ~/.local/share/openhamshack-atv/, named with the time, rx or tx, and the frequency, e.g. openhamshack-atv-20261004-175109-rx-1255.000MHz.png.

6.5 The spectrum's look

Settings › Display chooses the waterfall palette and turns automatic floor and contrast on or off. Ctrl+T switches between the dark and light themes.

6.6 Recording IQ, and playing it back

Record IQ, in the header of the receive band, records the receiving radio's raw samples — exactly what the decoder sees, in the radio's own format (16-bit from a Pluto, 8-bit with Tezuka's 8-bit receive) — as a SigMF pair: a .sigmf-data file of samples and a small .sigmf-meta file that says what they are, with the frequency, system, settings and picture format. Click it again to stop; otherwise it stops at the time or size limit in Settings › Radio (60 s and 4000 MB by default), whichever comes first, and it will not start unless the disk has room for all of it. 10 MS/s of 8-bit IQ is 20 MB a second, 16-bit 40 MB. Recordings go in ~/.local/share/openhamshack-atv/recordings/, named like ohsatv-20261005-123843Z-fm-1251.000MHz.

To play one back, set Settings › Radio › Radio to Recording (IQ file) and choose it under Recording to play, or run openhamshack-atv --play <file>.sigmf-meta. The recording goes through the same receiver in real time, taking its rate, system, settings and tuning from the file, in a loop if you choose. Playback never transmits. A recording made on a Pluto-family radio has the radio's own filters undone on playback, as live receive does (section 10).

7. Transmitting

Before you transmit on a real radio
  • An SDR's transmit output is not filtered: it carries aliases and harmonics. Put a band-pass or low-pass filter after it, and after any amplifier, before an antenna.
  • Check the band, the frequency and the bandwidth against your licence and the bandplan; check the power you will radiate (above 10 W EIRP the licence's EMF condition applies).
  • The first time, transmit into a dummy load through an attenuator, and watch it on a second receiver.

7.1 What has to be true before PTT works

The PTT button reads TRANSMIT LOCKED until every one of these is satisfied, and the first reason is written under it (hover for the rest):

  1. The licence box in Settings › Station is ticked.
  2. A callsign is set.
  3. For a real radio — a Pluto-family radio or a HackRF — Let a real radio transmit is ticked in Settings › Transmit (7.2).
  4. The radio is running, can transmit, and can tune this band.
  5. The signal fits the sample rate (the "What fits" card says ✓).
  6. The power is not above the ceiling.
  7. The band guard allows the frequency (7.6).

On the simulator, the band guard's refusals are shown as warnings instead — on a real radio this would be refused: … — so every mode can be tried; nothing leaves the computer.

7.2 Letting a real radio transmit

Let a real radio transmit. I have checked the band, the power and the antenna, and this is my licence. It is off when the program is installed, and only the Settings dialog can turn it on. Leave it off until the radio is connected to what you mean it to be connected to.

7.3 Power

The Power (TX gain) slider is attenuation in the SDR: 0 dB is the radio's full output, which is a few milliwatts (about +2.5 dBm from a Pluto at 23cm, −3 dBm at 6cm). It starts at −30 dB. Transmit gain ceiling in Settings › Transmit (default −10 dB) is the most the slider may reach; the red notch on the slider marks it. Set the ceiling for your amplifier's input, so that the slider cannot overdrive it. For a HackRF the same scale drives its 0–47 dB transmit gain.

7.4 Keying, and stopping

ToDo
TransmitClick PTT; click again to stop. Or hold Space: releasing it stops.
Stop at onceEsc, or close the window.

While transmitting the window has a red ON AIR frame, the frequency turns red, and the PTT bar shows how long you have been on and how long is left. If the window loses focus while you hold Space, transmit stops.

7.5 What stops a transmission by itself

  • Time-out — Settings › Transmit, default 10 minutes. A notice says Transmit stopped: the 10-minute time-out.
  • Watchdog — if the window stops responding for 2 seconds (the default), the engine stops transmitting.
  • A change that makes it wrong — tuning to a frequency the guard refuses, while keyed, unkeys.
  • The radio moving by itself — a Pluto-family radio whose transmit LO moves, or whose rate or receive LO moves and will not go back, unkeys at once (4.5).

The program never transmits when it starts, after a restart or reconnection, or after Settings are saved. Only PTT keys it.

7.6 The band guard: refusals and warnings

Every time the frequency, system or sample rate changes, the program checks the whole occupied signal — not just the carrier — against the UK allocation and the bandplan. Some things refuse transmit; others are warnings, shown in amber under PTT, because the UK's own ATV frequencies do them.

RefusedWarned
  • Any part of the signal outside a UK amateur allocation (on 6cm, outside one of the three blocks). The sentence says how far outside it would be.
  • On 23cm and 6cm, a carrier outside an ATV or all-mode segment of the bandplan. (70cm has no ATV segment; there the allocation alone is checked.)
  • The 23cm TV repeater outputs, 1300–1325 MHz (receive only).
  • A frequency the radio cannot tune.
  • Sidebands reaching into narrowband, beacon, satellite or repeater-output segments.
  • On 23cm, the signal overlapping Galileo E6 (1258.29–1299.21 MHz).
  • On 23cm, the signal in 1250–1290 MHz, where the RSGB asks for caution (primary users).
  • On 70cm, OUTSIDE THE BANDPLAN, as a red banner (8.1); and the 40 W ERP limit in 430–432 MHz.

Read the warnings before you transmit. They are why the guard does not refuse 1255, 1275 and 5665 MHz: those are the UK's simplex ATV frequencies, and a 17.8 MHz FM-ATV signal on them does reach into neighbouring segments.

7.7 Your ident

Your callsign, locator and second line are drawn into every frame of every source, for the whole transmission, and cannot be turned off. The UK licence (condition 6(21)) requires the station to be "clearly identifiable at all times"; a caption in vision is the safest reading of that for television. The ident box in the transmit panel shows exactly what goes out; with no callsign set it reads NO CALLSIGN and transmit is refused.

7.8 Half duplex, and what the picture shows while keyed

On a single real radio, receive pauses while you transmit — the link cannot carry both directions at video rates — and the picture switches to the TX preview, labelled as such, so you never mistake what you send for what you receive. The spectrum shows what you are sending, in red under a TX · THE SIGNAL BEING SENT · RECEIVE PAUSED label, and the waterfall carries on with those lines in grey between red edges, so afterwards you can still see which rows were your own transmission. At unkey both go back to receive. The exception is a LibreSDR or Pluto+ whose link is fast enough for both. With a HackRF transmitting, the other radio keeps receiving. On the simulator, your signal comes back through the loop.

7.9 Split: repeaters

Turn on SPLIT (under the frequency) to transmit on a different frequency from receive — a repeater's input while you watch its output. Click the TX frequency to type it. The 23cm repeater outputs (1300–1325 MHz) are receive-only; the inputs at 1248 and 1249 MHz are on the band card. There are no TV repeaters in Scotland; the nearest are GB3KM and GB3EY.

7.10 The transmit log

Every key-up and unkey is written, one line each, to ~/.local/share/openhamshack-atv/txlog.jsonl: the time (UTC), frequency, band, system, picture format, sample rate, radio, callsign, source and the gain actually used, whether the cable feed was on, the warnings that applied, and at unkey why it stopped and how long it lasted. A 70cm transmission is recorded with its OUTSIDE THE BANDPLAN warning.

7.11 The cable feed: 405 lines into a vintage set

A 405-line television tunes only the old BBC Band I channels, B1–B5 (41–68 MHz). Band I is not an amateur allocation, so OpenHamShack ATV will send there only by cable, from the radio through an attenuator into the set's aerial socket, and only like this:

  1. Open Settings › Transmit. In the red Cable feed box, tick the sentence first: The output goes by cable, through attenuation, into a receiver's aerial socket — never to an antenna. Then tick Cable feed on, and Save. Real hardware also needs Let a real radio transmit, as always.
  2. The radio goes to Band I (now in the band selector), AM-VSB and 405 lines, on B1. Pick the channel the set is tuned to from the channel list: B1 45.00, B2 51.75, B3 56.75, B4 61.75 or B5 66.75 MHz vision, the sound 3.5 MHz below each.
  3. A red CABLE FEED — NOT FOR ANTENNAS banner stays at the top of the transmit panel for as long as it is on. There is no power slider: every transmission uses the radio's least output (−89.75 dB on a Pluto; VGA 0 and the amplifier off on a HackRF).
  • Which radio: a HackRF tunes all five channels. A Pluto on Tezuka firmware tunes from 46.875 MHz, which is B2–B5 (B1's sound is at 41.5 MHz); a stock Pluto starts at 70 MHz and can send none. PTT says which channels the radio can send when it cannot send the one chosen.
  • Nothing else can be sent while it is on — not another format, not another frequency, not the amateur bands. Turn it off in Settings to go back on the air.
  • It is off every time the program starts, and switching it on or off while transmitting stops the transmission. Every transmission is in the log with "cableFeed": true.
  • How strong the least output is at 50 MHz has not been measured. Estimated from 70cm, a Pluto's is near −86 dBm, which a set may find weak; start with little attenuation, and add it if the set overloads. The attenuator is still the rule: it protects the set, and the air, if the radio does not do as it is told.

7.12 The cable feed on UHF: 625 lines and teletext into a 625-line set

A UK 625-line television tunes only UHF, so with the cable feed on there is a second band, UHF, for 625-line PAL (System I) with its FM sound 6 MHz above vision — and with teletext and subtitles, which a teletext set decodes. It is a cable feed like Band I: the same tick, the same banner, the least output, nothing else sent.

  1. Turn the cable feed on in Settings › Transmit as above. Then choose UHF in the band selector: the radio goes to AM-VSB, PAL, on E36 (591.25 MHz vision) — the old VCR channel, which a vintage set's spare button is often tuned to already.
  2. Other channels: E21–E48 except E38 (vision 8N + 303.25 MHz: E21 471.25 … E48 687.25). The band card has E21, E24, E28, E32, E36, E40, E44 and E48; type any other. Channels above 48 are mobile phone bands now, and 38 is radio astronomy, so they are refused.
  3. For teletext choose 10 MS/s or more in Settings › Radio (8 MS/s carries it with a narrower spectrum that a television may not decode; 5 MS/s cannot), switch Text on and pick the page folder as on air.
  • Which radio: every one tunes UHF — a stock Pluto from 325 MHz, a HackRF from 1 MHz.
  • Power: a Pluto's least output is estimated at about −86 to −82 dBm here, which may give a snowy picture and no teletext on a real set; a HackRF's is tens of dB more, and wants an attenuator of 30 dB or so. Try it on the bench first: docs/teletext-on-air-test.md, part E.
  • Band I stays 405 lines only, and teletext is refused there.

8. The bands

The band data comes from the RSGB 2026 band plan, the IARU Region 1 VHF Handbook 10.03, the Ofcom amateur licence (OFW611, February 2024) and the BATC wiki. Power limits are PEP for the Full licence unless stated.

Analogue ATV is no longer in the 70cm bandplan The RSGB removed fast-scan analogue TV from its 70cm plan on 4 December 2020, and IARU Region 1 says that from 1 January 2021 "Analogue ATV and SATV is no longer permitted in the 430-440 MHz band". Only narrow DATV around 437 MHz remains. The radio's own filters, undone

Getting FM colour through on the air (5 October 2026, LibreSDR to nano at 12.5 MS/s) turned up two filters in the way:

  • A filter another program had left behind. The LibreSDR still had a DATV program's FIR filter loaded, and it stripped the colour and sound from the transmission altogether. The driver now bypasses an FIR it finds enabled when it opens the radio, and turns it back on when it closes it.
  • The chip's own fixed filters. With that filter out of the path, colour came through, but the colour burst arrived at 0.73 of what was sent and the sound subcarrier 7–10 dB down. Every AD936x passes its samples through a chain of half-band filters that the RF bandwidth setting does not touch, and the last of them is 6 dB down at half the sample rate by design. So the top of every channel is cut: at 12.5 MS/s, transmitter and receiver together take about 2.3 dB off the 4.43 MHz colour and 7.8 dB off a 5.5 MHz sound subcarrier, and the same fractions of the sample rate apply at every rate.

OpenHamShack ATV now models that response from Analog Devices' own filter coefficients — the model matches both test radios' noise floors — and each radio undoes its own share, up to 6 dB, inside filters the receiver and transmitter already run, so it costs no processing. On the air with 0.1.7 the colour burst came in at 0.90 of what was sent (from 0.73), with the picture's SNR and the sound unharmed (46.9 dB SINAD). The receiving half recovered exactly what the model predicted; the transmitting half recovered less, because that LibreSDR's transmitter is about 1 dB worse than its model.

  • Each radio undoes only its own filters: a Pluto receiving a HackRF recovers its own share and no more. With someone else's FIR still running, no correction is applied.
  • IQ recordings stay as the radio delivered them; playback of one made on a Pluto-family radio applies the receive correction (6.6).
  • Boosting the top of the channel raises the transmitter's images out there by the same amount — at 12.5 MS/s the sound subcarrier's, 7 MHz out, from about −42 to −39 dBc. No radio is steered to transmit at 12.5 MS/s, but it is one more reason for the filter after the radio (section 7).
  • Still unexplained: the upper sideband arrives 2–3 dB weaker than the lower. Swapping the radios' roles did not move it to one radio; it is most likely the antennas or the bench path.

When the computer or the link cannot keep up

  • A rate above what the link is reported to sustain is marked in Settings and on the "What fits" card. If samples are actually dropped, the card says Samples are being dropped: the link cannot keep up with this rate.
  • skipped in the status strip counts frames the decoder dropped to keep up. Measured on a Ryzen 9 6900HX, FM-ATV receive at 20 MS/s uses about 1.7 cores at a full 25 frames a second; a live video file looped back through the simulator at 20 MS/s drops the monitor to about 17 frames a second (the transmitter keeps time). On the test laptop, an i5-1135G7, an off-air AM-VSB colour recording at 10 MS/s plays with the window open at 24 frames a second with nothing skipped; the hardest case, Mini-MAC received at 16 MS/s, skips a picture about every ten seconds without losing any sound. A Raspberry Pi has not been measured.
  • When the computer falls behind the radio, whole 40 ms frames are skipped so the picture keeps its line and field timing; the "What fits" card says so for ten seconds after each.

405 lines (System A)

On AM-VSB the transmit panel's Format offers 625 PAL or 405 lines: the British 405-line system of 1936–1985 (CCIR System A), the way the BBC sent it — positive modulation (the sync pulses at no carrier, white at full), a 3 MHz black-and-white picture, and AM sound 3.5 MHz below the vision carrier. It is a standard: a vintage 405-line set receives it, and so does another OpenHamShack ATV.

  • It fits a USB Pluto whole. The picture's sideband lies between the sound and vision carriers, so the whole signal is 4.3 MHz wide — at 5 MS/s, the rate a USB Pluto can transmit, you send the full 3 MHz picture and its sound. The "What fits" card shows 405 MONO and −3.5 AM.
  • The receiver recognises it. With Recognise the picture format on receive on, a receiver set for 625 lines that finds 405 on the air (or the other way round) switches by itself, with a notice; what you transmit does not change. The simulator has a 405-line station on 1287.5 MHz to try it on.
  • Measured in the simulator (test card, 1 kHz tone): the picture holds lock down to about 14 dB carrier-to-noise at 5 MS/s (10 dB at 10 MS/s), is clean from about 16 dB, and the sound is clear from about 10 dB — the same as a 625-line mono picture at that rate, which has no sound at all.
  • The test card has its own gratings for 405 lines: 0.6 to 3 MHz.

11. Transmitting with a HackRF

A HackRF One carries 8-bit samples at up to 20 MS/s over USB 2, so it can send standard FM-ATV and colour AM-VSB with sound from a USB device, which a USB Pluto cannot. OpenHamShack ATV uses it for transmit only, beside the radio that receives — a Pluto, a LibreSDR, or the simulator.

Setting it up

  1. Install libhackrf0 (recommended by the package, so normally already there). hackrf_info, from the hackrf package, is a quick check that the system sees it.
  2. Settings › Radio › Transmitter: choose HackRF One (transmit only). Fill in the serial number if you have more than one.
  3. Settings › Transmit: tick Let a real radio transmit — a HackRF is real hardware, even when the receiver is the simulator.

How it behaves

  • Split operation is full duplex: two radios, so receive carries on while you transmit.
  • The HackRF tunes 1 MHz–6 GHz at 2–20 MS/s; the receiving radio runs at the same sample rate.
  • Power: the power slider maps onto the HackRF's 0–47 dB transmit gain. Its 14 dB RF amplifier is a separate switch in Settings, off by default. Output falls with frequency and is low at 5.8 GHz: an external PA is needed there. A HackRF needs a filter after it, like any SDR.
  • It is quiet until you key: gain is raised only once samples are flowing, and unkeying cuts the gain at once even if the USB stream has stalled.
  • Underruns: if the computer falls behind, the HackRF sends a gap, and the far end sees a break in the picture. These are counted and shown on the "What fits" card: HackRF underruns: n — the computer is not keeping up. They are never hidden.
  • Receiving at the same rate: if the receiver is a USB Pluto, it cannot carry 20 MS/s and will drop samples while receiving, and the window says so. Full-width receive needs a LibreSDR or Pluto+ on Ethernet.

The device chip at the top shows TX HackRF · receive … when this is in use.

12. 6cm on a Pluto

A stock ADALM-Pluto uses the AD9363 transceiver, which tunes 325–3800 MHz. 6cm (5650–5850 MHz) is outside that. When the radio cannot reach a band, the band button is greyed and says why; tuning there is refused with the same sentence.

Reaching 6cm

  • An AD9361 or AD9364 board — such as the LibreSDR — tunes 70–6000 MHz as it is.
  • A Pluto set to AD9364 mode. In a shell on the Pluto (ssh root@192.168.2.1): 
    fw_setenv attr_name compatible
fw_setenv attr_val ad9364
fw_setenv compatible ad9364     # firmware 0.32 and later only
reboot
    Afterwards the Pluto tunes 70–6000 MHz. Analog Devices do not guarantee the AD9363's performance outside its range, and units vary. This changes your radio's settings; it is your decision.

OpenHamShack ATV reads what the radio can tune when it opens it — the radio's own reported range, then the driver's model, then a test tune — and says This radio tunes 70–6000 MHz in Settings › Radio.

Low output, deaf receiver: PA and LNA

  • Output at 5760 MHz is about −3 dBm (K0SM's measurement on a Pluto). To reach even 1 W you need more than 33 dB of amplification.
  • The receiver's noise figure there is about 10 dB. An LNA in front (with a band-pass filter) makes the difference between hearing nothing and a usable station.
  • In practice, an external PA and LNA are needed on 6cm. The band card says so.

14. NICAM stereo

With standard PAL the program can send, and always receives, NICAM-728 digital stereo sound, as UK television added to its picture. It is switched on under Transmit › Stereo. It does not replace anything: the analogue sound carries on beside it, with the two channels mixed.

14.1 Where it fits

AM-VSB10 MS/s and up. The vision carrier is moved a little further from the middle of the band to make room; the "What fits" card shows the new layout.
FM-ATV16 MS/s at 3.2 MHz p-p or less; 20 MS/s at the IARU's 7 MHz p-p. Not with the 6.5 MHz sound subcarrier, which sits on NICAM's 6.19–6.92 MHz: choose 6.0.

Where the rate has no room, the switch stays on but nothing extra is sent, and the transmit panel says why in amber. Not with 405 lines, nor with the narrow formats (Mini-MAC, narrow colour PAL).

14.2 What is sent and heard

The signal is System I's, from ETSI EN 300 163: 728 kbit/s of DQPSK 6.552 MHz above the vision carrier, 32 kHz 14-bit sound companded to 10 bits, J.17 pre-emphasis. On AM-VSB it is 20 dB below the vision carrier, as broadcast; on FM-ATV (which has no NICAM standard) its subcarrier deviates the carrier about as much as the sound subcarrier does.

What goes out depends on the Sound choice: with Tone, the stereo test - the tone on the left and twice its frequency on the right (1 and 2 kHz), so you can tell the channels apart; with File, the file's own two channels; with Mic, the microphone on both.

Receiving is automatic: wherever the receive band can hold NICAM the program listens for it, and plays it in stereo while it is cleaner than the analogue sound. The NICAM badge at the top says what you are hearing:

BadgeMeans

MONO / DUALA NICAM mono or dual-language transmission: the first channel is played.

How far it reaches: on AM-VSB NICAM lasts about as long as the picture (error-free to 20 dB CNR, failing near 12, where the picture is grainy but locked). On FM-ATV it needs a good signal - about 20 dB CNR - long after which the picture and the analogue sound still work; the program switches to the analogue sound by itself.

The simulator's own PAL stations send NICAM, so it can be tried without a radio (Settings › Simulator, "NICAM stereo from the stations").

17. Files and the command line

WhatWhere
Settings~/.config/openhamshack-atv/config.json (or $OHSATV_CONF)

The program is openhamshack-atv; the same options work as python3 -m ohsatv from a source checkout.

openhamshack-atv                          the window
openhamshack-atv --headless               the engine alone, a status line a second (never transmits)
openhamshack-atv --headless --seconds 10  ... for ten seconds
openhamshack-atv --device sim|pluto|file  use this radio for this run only
openhamshack-atv --play FILE.sigmf-meta   play an IQ recording through the receiver (never transmits)
openhamshack-atv --band 23cm --system fm  start on this band and system
openhamshack-atv --no-audio               run without a sound card

Options that override the settings for a run are not saved.
